# The Zodiac Standard

*Recognition is inherited. Demand is not.*

Twelve tokens carry the names of the twelve zodiac signs. They were deployed on 5 July 2024, and this registry catalogues them. This essay examines them as a store-of-value candidacy: it states exactly what the records inherit from the zodiac's history, what they do not inherit, and what evidence would settle the difference. It makes the case, then the case against at the same strength, then puts the instrument on the table. Nothing on this page is advice, and nothing on it asks for faith.

*Read the argument*

---

## Part I. The object, and the claim.

Three objects appear in this essay, and they are not interchangeable.

A **sign record** is a specific token: one mint address per sign on Solana, created 5 July 2024, each with a published origin receipt. The **twelve-record set** is all of them considered together. The **registry** is this catalogue: a static public file, an open-source SDK, an archive, and the disclosures around them. Demand, holder concentration, administrative control, and liquidity can differ sharply from sign to sign, so evidence on this page is reported at the sign level and the set level, and neither is treated as proof about the other. The registry itself is not the asset and holds no rights over it; it is a record anyone can fork.

The claim, exactly. This essay does not claim the zodiac is a store of value; a culture is not an instrument. It does not claim these twelve tokens are one; the scoreboard in Part VI is mostly zeros, and says so. The claim is narrower. The twelve signs are a rare kind of vocabulary — old, fixed in number, widely recognized, renewed on a public calendar by publishers with no connection to any token — and inheriting that vocabulary may reduce one of the costs every new memetic asset must pay: teaching strangers its symbols. If that advantage is real, it should eventually appear as unaffiliated parties — builders, publishers, holders — repeatedly selecting these specific records when they could select a substitute, or nothing. That proposition is falsifiable, and it is under test on this page.

The stakes, stated plainly. If independent parties converge on these addresses, this registry becomes the durable reference point for a small, odd, real corner of public records. If they do not, the zodiac's history remains what it has always been — a culture, doing fine without a ledger — and these remain twelve tokens with unusually thorough documentation. Either result will be published here.

## Part II. What is inherited — and what is not.

People have organized meaning around the sky for a very long time, and that part of the inheritance is genuinely old: Babylonian star catalogues and omen series reach back into the second millennium BCE. The zodiac this essay concerns is younger and more precise. The fixed scheme — twelve equal signs of thirty degrees — appears in late Babylonian astronomy around the fifth century BCE, roughly two thousand four hundred years ago. It does not predate money; coined money was circulating in Lydia more than a century before the twelve were fixed. An earlier draft of this page claimed more age than the record supports. The correction is made here, and the argument loses nothing by it: two and a half millennia is enough.

What the record does support is transmission. The twelve passed from Babylonian astronomy into Greek horoscopic practice, through Ptolemy, into Arabic astronomy, back into Latin Europe, through Renaissance print, into the newspaper column, and onto the phone. The twelve-name structure persisted across every hop; the calculations, meanings, iconography, and institutions around it changed repeatedly. That is the precise shape of the durability on offer: a stable vocabulary, not a static doctrine. The empires that carried it are gone and the languages it first spoke are dead, and the names are still in daily print.

Recognition today is measurable, with its scope stated. In a 2022 U.S. survey, roughly [nine in ten adults selected a sign](https://today.yougov.com/entertainment/articles/42292-one-four-americans-say-they-believe-astrology) when shown the twelve — a prompted, single-country result, cited as such. [Pew finds](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/05/21/3-in-10-americans-consult-astrology-tarot-cards-or-fortune-tellers/) about three in ten U.S. adults consult astrology at least yearly, concentrated among younger adults and women. Comparable global recognition data are not presented here, and much of the world's astrological practice runs on other systems entirely: Vedic astrology uses a sidereal zodiac; the Chinese zodiac is a different scheme. Every claim on this page is scoped to where the Western twelve actually circulate.

The renewal is also real, and its mechanism matters more than its poetry. Western astrology has recurring publishing occasions — daily horoscopes, monthly sign changes, birthdays, widely covered celestial events — and they are produced by independent publishers, app companies, and creators, each spending their own money for their own audiences. No single institution finances the vocabulary's upkeep, and none of the spending has any connection to a token. Large consumer platforms have tested the same attention: [Tinder reported](https://www.tinderpressroom.com/2026-03-12-Tinder-Debuts-Inaugural-Product-Keynote-Tinder-Sparks-2026-Start-Something-New) that an internal early test of its Astrology Mode showed a nearly 20% increase in Likes sent by women on participating profiles; [Spotify](https://newsroom.spotify.com/2019-02-01/spotify-celebrates-new-cosmic-playlists-with-readings-from-guest-astrologer/) and [Netflix](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/tv/tv-news/netflix-astrology-hub-zodiac-sign-1236349558/) have shipped astrology features; [Co-Star reports](https://www.statista.com/statistics/1451873/costar-registered-users/) thirty million registered accounts. These are product experiments — evidence of perceived audience interest, not of institutional belief, and not of durable demand.

Now the other half, which essays in this genre habitually skip. The records inherit a recognized vocabulary. They do not inherit demand: no measure on this page shows anyone wanting a sign token because they know their sign. They do not inherit scarcity: the names are fixed at twelve, but tokens wearing the names are unlimited, and Part III returns to that at full strength. They do not inherit canonicity or legitimacy: nothing about the culture selects these mint addresses over any substitute, and the culture's institutions do not know these records exist. And they inherit no claim on astrology's revenues: the horoscope economy pays writers and app companies, and nothing routes a cent of it to a token.

The twelve-sign vocabulary predates these records by roughly 2,400 years. The records inherit recognition of the names — nothing else. The candidacy begins only if unaffiliated parties repeatedly choose the same addresses when they could choose others, or none. The rest of this page is about that narrower proposition.

## Part III. What a durable candidate requires.

Every store of value includes a solved coordination problem: strangers holding a thing partly because they expect other strangers to keep treating it as worth holding. But coordination is necessary, not sufficient. Durability also depends on supply integrity, enforceability, liquidity, custody, governance, and the availability of substitutes. Reducing the phenomenon to belief alone flatters candidates that have nothing else, so this essay will not do it — even where crypto's own voices do.

> "Gold's price is not derived from its industrial or utility value. Just a pure belief system."
>
> — [CZ, founder of Binance · October 2025](https://x.com/cz_binance/status/1983514879643292126)

The strong version is wrong. Gold is not pure belief: it is scarce by physics, costly to extract, worn as jewelry, vaulted by central banks, liquid everywhere, and recognized by centuries of law and custom. Belief is one layer of the premium, not the whole tower. What survives the correction is narrower and still useful: some meaningful share of gold's value is coordination around a durable symbol — people holding it because they expect others to keep treating it as worth holding, a habit millennia deep. Gold's history is not decoration on its price; it is the accumulated proof that the agreement survives wars and centuries, which is what a new candidate cannot buy at any price.

Bitcoin demonstrated a complementary set of properties: that scarcity can be enforced by consensus rather than physics, that settlement can be digital and permissionless, and that a monetary agreement can bootstrap from nothing if the rules are fixed and auditable by anyone. What it could not import was history, so it spent fifteen years accumulating one, surviving every obituary written about it along the way.

Gold and bitcoin are used here as comparison cases, not as a pedigree. They illustrate different sources of credibility — accumulated history, enforced rules. Neither case establishes that a token inherits cultural credibility by wearing a culture's name. Whether that transfer can happen at all is the open question of this essay, and the table below keeps the comparison honest by marking the unproven cells unproven.

One newer consideration belongs in the theory, stated as the hypothesis it is. Generative systems are collapsing the cost of producing new symbols, mascots, and whole movements' worth of content. One hypothesis — held here, not proven here — is that this abundance raises the relative salience of documented provenance: symbols with long, verifiable human histories may matter more when synthetic ones are free. This essay does not claim that mechanism creates demand for these records. It claims only that if provenance gains salience, the zodiac holds an unusual amount of it, and a registry's contribution is documentation.

The token market of the last decade supplies a large observational sample: thousands of symbols proposed as coordination points, most now inactive. It is not a controlled experiment, and this essay offers no failure rate — that would require a defined cohort, observation window, and death criterion. What the sample does support is a set of recurring failure patterns, stated here as four hypotheses about symbolic durability. The twelve avoid each in a specific, checkable way — and clearing all four eliminates four risks; it does not establish demand for any token.

**Permission.** A coin built on a brand, a character, or a celebrity borrows a symbol with a legal owner attached, and carries that owner as counterparty. No single party owns the twelve-sign vocabulary. Particular marks, artworks, and implementations can still be protected — the claim is about the underlying names, not everything wearing them.

**Offense.** Sacred vocabulary makes an asset unwelcome to some audience by construction. The sign names are less entangled with any single proprietor or organized faith than most symbols strong enough to build on. They are not universally welcome — some faiths object to astrology itself, and Part VIII takes that seriously — and no global measure of objection is offered here.

**Bootstrap.** The most self-aware corner of the memecoin era understood that belief itself is the asset — that a community choosing its agreement deliberately is doing openly what every market does tacitly. The insight is real, and acting on it from a standing start means manufacturing a myth and holding it up by continuous effort: content, ritual, recruitment, forever. The base rate is brutal. The zodiac's recognition needs no manufacturing — the vocabulary renews on its own calendar, maintained by publishers who have never heard of this registry. But the inheritance stops at the vocabulary. Everything address-level — recognition of a mint, trust in its administration, willingness to hold it — is uncovered. The congregation exists; nothing about it has chosen this ledger.

**Depth.** Many token narratives depend on repeated variation of a single premise, and attention for them must be purchased for as long as they live. Astrology differs in a specific way: independent publishers already produce recurring material across horoscopes, natal charts, compatibility, sign seasons, and celestial events — on their own schedules, for their own audiences. That breadth belongs to the culture, not to this registry or its operator's platform, and it establishes no demand for a token. What it changes is narrower: a sign token's subject matter does not run out.

Which leaves the concession that keeps this essay honest: **the twelve signs are scarce; tokens named after them are not.** Anyone can deploy another Leo this afternoon, and eventually someone will. Bitcoin's scarcity is enforced by consensus; no code on any chain prevents a thirteenth Leo. The openness runs deeper than copycats: the monetization surface around the zodiac — apps, NFTs, wrappers, brands, competing registries — is unlimited. A closed vocabulary is not a closed market. What a closed vocabulary does provide is a narrow label space: coordination is cheaper on twelve names everyone already knows than on an open genre where every launch dilutes the last. Cheaper is not automatic, and labels select names, not mints. The wager of this registry is that convergence happens, and happens on these records — and Part VI puts that wager under published criteria and exclusions instead of leaving it as rhetoric.

Periodic Ophiuchus stories — the recurring "thirteenth sign" news cycle — show that the popular twelve-sign convention remains resilient under challenge. They do not establish why it persists or whether that persistence benefits any token.

## Part IV. Why these addresses — or none.

The hardest question the candidacy faces is not whether the zodiac matters. It is why mattering would select these mints. Names are not addresses; a culture cannot endorse a contract. So the mechanisms have to be named, and each carries its own weakness.

**Dated provenance.** These records have a continuous public history: origin receipts dated 5 July 2024, a registry file, an archive that has not broken. No earlier dated public registry of one-token-per-sign records is known to this registry; that is a falsifiable priority claim — producing an earlier one defeats it — not the result of an exhaustive search. Provenance makes a record easy to verify and cite. It does not compel anyone to cite it.

**Low integration cost.** The registry file is static and public; the [SDK](https://zodiacs.org/sdk/) is open-source; the records are machine-readable. A builder who wants sign records can adopt these in an afternoon. The same openness means a builder can fork the format and point it at different mints in the same afternoon.

**A narrow label space.** Twelve known names give coordination somewhere obvious to land — but the step from name to address is precisely the step the candidacy has to earn, and nothing in the culture takes it automatically.

**What could defeat all three.** A better-capitalized issuer. An exchange or wallet shipping its own sign records to a larger audience. A future registry that out-earns this one on the same criteria — which the criteria in Part VI would then crown, by design. Or the most likely outcome on current evidence: no convergence at all.

What holding a record is today should be stated as carefully. Each sign has a public record with a published address; a Leo can hold *Leo*. What the chain verifies is the holding, not the Leo. Anyone can buy all twelve signs; a Leo can hold none. The token is a badge, not a credential — a tradable symbol of affiliation, like a jersey, which never proved fandom either and never needed to. This essay will not pretend a wallet proves a birthday. Whether holders in fact use the records as public badges is an empirical question the evidence file will answer over time, not a property the essay can assert. Holder-gated functions exist today on the operator-affiliated consumer surfaces ([Astrofolio](https://astrofolio.xyz/)); the candidacy's own exclusions discount those, and unaffiliated integrations stand at zero on the scoreboard. Proposed functions are not part of the claim. Some holders may hold in the expectation that others will want the records later; that motive exists in every memetic asset, it is not evidence for the candidacy, and this page does not appeal to it.

The commerce around the culture belongs in the record with its labels on. [Allied Market Research estimated](https://www.alliedmarketresearch.com/press-release/astrology-market.html) the global astrology market at $12.8 billion in 2021 and forecast $22.8 billion by 2031 — an estimate and a forecast, cited as such. [Sensor Tower estimated](https://sensortower.com/blog/astrology-apps-2019-revenue-downloads) that the ten highest-earning U.S. astrology apps took $39.7 million in consumer spending in 2019, up 64.7 percent from the prior year's top ten. [Co-Star raised a $15 million Series A](https://www.axios.com/2021/04/14/astrology-app-co-star-raises-15-million-funding) for an app that sells horoscopes. All of it was paid for readings, personalization, and entertainment — a service consumed, not an asset held. A paying culture establishes commercial activity around the vocabulary; it establishes nothing about demand for these records. And [Pew finds](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/05/21/3-in-10-americans-consult-astrology-tarot-cards-or-fortune-tellers/) most people who consult astrology do it mostly for fun and almost none rely on it for major decisions — evidence that engagement is recreational, with no established effect, in either direction, on demand for a token.

## Part VI. The candidacy: criteria, exclusions, and the test.

This registry claims neither scarcity nor, yet, canonicity. It has no formal authority, so any claim to be canonical depends entirely on later independent adoption. What zodiacs.org holds today is dated provenance and a head start: a continuous public record since 5 July 2024, machine-readable addresses, an [open-source SDK](https://zodiacs.org/sdk/) any builder can verify against. Until better evidence accumulates, "canonical" names the objective, not the fact.

Five kinds of evidence count, each measured separately, each with a dated pass condition and a dated loss condition, and no causal relationship among them assumed: independent integrations that read this registry rather than another; third parties citing these addresses unprompted; holder dispersion widening; liquidity that survives quiet months; an archive that stays continuous through adverse ones.

The exclusions carry as much weight as the thresholds, because four of the five metrics could be manufactured. Only activity attributable to unaffiliated parties counts toward any pass condition. Integrations, citations, claims, wallets, and liquidity connected to the operator, related parties, paid placement, grants, rebates, coordinated campaigns, or operator-controlled products — including the Astrofolio consumer surfaces — are reported separately and excluded, and the evidence file records the exclusion decision for each observation. Prompted mentions are recorded as prompted. Archive continuity, the fifth criterion, is under operator control and is therefore weighted as the weakest form of evidence: its absence would end the candidacy; its presence proves only diligence.

## Part VIII. The case against.

An argument that hides its weaknesses is an advertisement. Seven objections, each with its evidence and its implication left standing.

**"Belief in the sign is not demand for the record."** Correct, and it is the actual bet. No public measure presented on this page estimates current demand for the records; the spending in Part IV concerns services consumed, not assets held; and the substitutes are infinitely reproducible. The nearest precedent cuts against: football fan tokens — durable tribal identity, closed sets, official issuance — have exhibited severe drawdowns from their peaks across the sector's own published retrospectives. The structural difference is narrow: fan tokens were centrally issued against promised utility that largely failed to arrive; the twelve are permissionless records that promise nothing — no votes, no perks, no yield. Less to fail to deliver, and less to point to. The implication is stated without a hedge: the evidence on this page establishes a falsifiable candidacy. It does not estimate a probability of success, and it does not claim the probability is high.

**"If the audience wanted this, it would already exist."** The existing crypto-astrology overlap is a partial test with a negative result, and it counts against the thesis. What remains is narrower: the preregistered test exposes a defined mainstream cohort to a specified one-tap flow — something the evidence register shows that cohort has not had — and if nothing moves, the friction explanation is spent, and this page will say so. The implication: the thesis currently rests on an untested mechanism, and the test date is published.

**"The registry appointed itself."** It did. Some registries begin without formal authority and become authoritative through later use; most never do. Legitimacy here can only be earned the slow way — publish verifiable facts, keep the archive continuous, never sell the position, be right for years — and the same criteria that would crown this record would crown a competitor that out-earned it. The implication: self-appointment is not disqualifying, and it is also not evidence.

**"This is a memecoin with a literature review."** The token is memetic — priced by attention and belief — and the literature review does not change that. The remaining question is whether a fixed vocabulary changes address-level coordination, and that question is open. The implication: the honest version of this page's thesis survives the insult intact, because the insult and the thesis describe the same object at different levels of care.

**"You are describing a security and insisting you are not."** The registry's answer is behavioral and auditable: it issues nothing, sells nothing, custodies nothing, and promises nothing — no yield, no buyback, no roadmap of managerial effort for a holder to rely on. The catalogue analogy has a stated limit: this operator holds the catalogued assets and develops adjacent products, which is exactly why the [disclosures](https://zodiacs.org/disclosure/) exist and why Part VI's exclusions discount operator-adjacent activity. The implication: the page confines itself to publishing facts and labeling arguments, and asks to be judged on whether those registers ever blur.

**"One operator runs the registry, the companion app, and holds the assets it catalogues."** True, attested, and published, dated, in the operator's own words. The history in Part V adds the uncomfortable version: the earliest affiliated language around these tokens was investment sloganeering, and the registry register came later. The structural mitigation is that this registry is built to be replaceable — static file, forkable SDK, mirrored archive, symmetric criteria. Public files reduce the technical cost of routing around an operator; they do not guarantee anyone would migrate, and the implication stands: concentration of operation is a live risk the reader should price, not a solved one.

**"The essay is the mythology; the instrument is the asset."** The sharpest objection, and the only one prose cannot answer. Most readers cannot reconstruct control rights from a block explorer, and should not have to. The answer is Part VII, and the implication is an instruction: weigh the table more than the prose. Where the table and the prose ever disagree, the table wins.

## Part IX. What this page claims, and what it does not.

The title of this page names an ambition, the way "canonical" does — an endpoint the evidence has not reached. So the conclusion is the claim at its true size.

Inherited recognition may reduce one coordination cost for one kind of asset. It does not select these addresses, and it does not create demand for them. Whether unaffiliated parties converge on these records is not a matter of argument; it is a matter of record, and the record is being kept in public — criteria published, exclusions defined, test preregistered, result binding either way. Two years of continuous documentation is the head start. The scoreboard is mostly zeros. Both facts are on this page on purpose.

The registry's contribution is not conviction; it is documentation. The culture does not need these records, and never did. Whether these records come to matter is exactly what the test is for.

# Appendix — The technical record

## A. Reading the instrument.

The column legend for the instrument table in Part VII:

- **Supply** — total and circulating, per sign
- **Mint authority** — status per sign (retained / renounced), address if retained
- **Freeze authority** — status per sign
- **Holder concentration** — top-10 wallet share per sign, dated
- **Liquidity** — venue, pool addresses, LP ownership and any locks
- **Bridge** — mechanism for Base representations, custody model, what fails if the bridge fails
- **Treasury and insider holdings** — project-controlled wallets, amounts, any lockups or vesting, disposal policy
- **Registry continuity** — governance of the record itself; what survives if the operating entity disappears (static registry file, forkable SDK, mirrored archive)

Statuses are three-valued and mean what they say: **verified** (a stranger can check the receipt), **operator-attested** (the operator states it, dated, not independently verified), **pending** (neither, and labeled so). Pending is never dressed as verified.

## B. Why Solana. Why Base.

The native records were deployed on Solana. The relevant selection factors are technical and practical: transaction cost, confirmation behavior, consumer wallet availability, existing liquidity venues, and the operator's existing infrastructure at deployment time. Official bridged representations extend each sign to Base through the Wormhole lock-and-mint contracts listed per sign in the instrument. The Base representations trace to the specified Solana mints through those bridge contracts, and their validity depends on the bridge's stated contracts and trust assumptions. If the bridge fails, the native Solana records stand unaffected; the bridged representations are conveniences, not the record.

## C. Methodology and evidence.

The Pulse reads open sources on a published schedule: encyclopedia pageviews from the Wikimedia API (live, with a weekly committed snapshot as fallback) and search interest from public Trends data, with the comparison set and its limits stated inline. Pageviews measure curiosity, not bids; the essay never uses them as demand.

The candidacy scoreboard and the one-tap test read their committed JSON registers, which carry measurement definitions, capture times, exclusions, and raw captures in the published [evidence file](https://zodiacs.org/thesis/thesis-candidacy-evidence.json). Every observation carries an independence classification (unsolicited-unaffiliated, unsolicited-unknown, prompted, campaign, affiliated) and an economic-interest classification; only unsolicited-unaffiliated observations count toward pass conditions, and each exclusion decision is recorded. The public-history events in Part V carry the same schema — post identifiers, capture times, artifact grades A–E, live/deleted status — in the same register family. The test is preregistered: criteria, cohort, thresholds, and dates were fixed and published before the test began, and the result publishes either way.
