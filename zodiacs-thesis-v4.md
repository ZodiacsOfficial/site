# The Zodiac Standard

*Recognition is inherited. Demand is not.*

An argument about twelve very old names and twelve very new records — made at full strength, then argued against at the same strength, with the receipts on the table. Nothing on this page is advice, and nothing on it asks for faith.

*Read the argument*

---

## Part I. The accident.

On 17 February 2025, one of the loudest accounts on the internet bought the wrong token. Argentina's president had just endorsed a coin called LIBRA and the feed was on fire; somewhere in the scramble, a man with millions of followers bought a different Libra — a token named for the seventh sign of the zodiac, deployed the previous July — announced he'd been had, predicted his purchase would go to zero, and then, a few posts later, was telling his audience he'd always been a huge astrology guy.

Nothing about that afternoon proves anything. That is exactly why it opens this essay. An accidental purchase is not adoption; a joke about zero is not analysis. But the accident points straight at the question this page exists to ask: what happens when the oldest naming system still in daily use collides with the newest kind of public record — and is any version of that collision worth taking seriously as a store of value? The answer here is *maybe, under conditions this page can name* — and most of those conditions have not happened yet. That answer is worth your next twenty minutes precisely because it is not a yes.

The objects in question are easy to state. Twelve tokens, one per sign, each a specific mint on Solana with a published origin receipt dated 5 July 2024. One at a time, they are twelve separate instruments. Together, a set. Around them sits this registry — a static file, an open [SDK](https://zodiacs.org/sdk/), an archive — which issues nothing, owns no rights over any of it, and can be forked by anyone with an afternoon. Evidence about one sign is not evidence about the set; evidence about the set is not evidence for this registry. This page keeps the three apart, and you should too.

The claim is narrow, and it is not that the zodiac is money. Cultures are not instruments. The claim: the twelve signs are a vocabulary almost nobody on the Western calendar has to be taught — old, fixed in number, renewed daily by an industry that has no idea these records exist — and inheriting that vocabulary spares a new asset exactly one cost: teaching strangers its symbols. Every other cost still stands. If the spared cost matters, strangers with no connection to this registry will keep choosing these records over substitutes, on their own, and the scoreboard below will say so. If it doesn't, the scoreboard will keep reading zero, and the zodiac will keep not needing a ledger. Either way, it gets written down here.

## Part II. Old names.

Start with what is genuinely durable, because it is real. Every currency in circulation when the twelve signs were fixed is dust. The empires that carried them are gone; the languages they were first written in are dead. The names are still in the morning paper.

The fixing itself is datable — and younger than this essay once claimed. Star catalogues and sky-omen lists run back into the second millennium BCE, but the scheme itself, twelve equal signs of thirty degrees, appears in Babylonian astronomy around the fifth century BCE. Call it 2,400 years, beginning a century or so *after* the first coins. An earlier draft of this page said five thousand years and "older than money." Both were wrong, and the correction costs nothing: twenty-four centuries with the receipts intact is enough.

What those centuries actually show is transmission. The twelve crossed into Greek horoscopes, through Ptolemy, into Arabic astronomy, back into Latin Europe, into print, into the Sunday paper, onto the phone: carved, printed, syndicated, dialed, downloaded. The names never changed. Everything else did — the mathematics, the meanings, the pictures, the institutions. What survived is a vocabulary, not a doctrine. A vocabulary is exactly the part a token could borrow.

How many people hold the vocabulary today is measurable, if the scope stays honest. Shown the twelve, roughly [nine in ten U.S. adults pick theirs](https://today.yougov.com/entertainment/articles/42292-one-four-americans-say-they-believe-astrology) — a prompted, single-country result, cited as one. [Pew finds](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/05/21/3-in-10-americans-consult-astrology-tarot-cards-or-fortune-tellers/) about three in ten U.S. adults consult astrology at least yearly, skewing younger and female. Global figures are not offered here, because good ones don't exist and much of the world runs other systems entirely — Vedic practice is sidereal; the Chinese zodiac is a different scheme. Scoped claims only.

The upkeep is the strange part. The vocabulary's maintenance is distributed and voluntary: horoscope desks, app studios, creators — each paying their own way, for their own audiences, coordinating with no one, none of them aware these records exist. Attention arrives on the calendar's schedule — dailies, sign changes, birthdays, the occasional eclipse taking over the feed. Occasions, not guarantees. The big platforms have run the experiment themselves: [Tinder reported](https://www.tinderpressroom.com/2026-03-12-Tinder-Debuts-Inaugural-Product-Keynote-Tinder-Sparks-2026-Start-Something-New) a near-20% lift in Likes in an internal early test of its Astrology Mode; [Spotify](https://newsroom.spotify.com/2019-02-01/spotify-celebrates-new-cosmic-playlists-with-readings-from-guest-astrologer/) and [Netflix](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/tv/tv-news/netflix-astrology-hub-zodiac-sign-1236349558/) shipped astrology features; [Co-Star reports](https://www.statista.com/statistics/1451873/costar-registered-users/) thirty million registered accounts. Product experiments, every one — proof that somebody in a growth meeting believed the audience was there. Not proof the audience wants anything held.

Now subtract what none of this buys, because this is where essays like this one usually start lying. The records inherit a recognized vocabulary — and nothing else. Not demand: no number on this page shows anyone wanting a sign token because they know their sign. Not scarcity: the names stop at twelve; tokens wearing them don't, and Part III faces that squarely. Not canonicity: nothing in the culture selects these mints over any copy, and the culture's institutions don't know these mints exist. Not revenue: the horoscope economy pays writers and app stores, and none of it routes to a token.

Twenty-four centuries of names. Two years of records. The bridge between them is unbuilt, and whether anyone builds it is the rest of this page.

## Part III. What survival takes.

A store of value is, at minimum, a crowd that keeps agreeing: strangers holding a thing because they expect other strangers to keep treating it as worth holding. That much is common to metal, land, and code. It is also nowhere near the whole job — supply has to hold, rules have to bind, markets have to stay liquid, custody has to work, and substitutes have to keep losing. Belief-only theories flatter assets that have nothing else, so this page won't run one. Crypto's most famous voice does:

> "Gold's price is not derived from its industrial or utility value. Just a pure belief system."
>
> — [CZ, founder of Binance · October 2025](https://x.com/cz_binance/status/1983514879643292126)

He is wrong in an instructive way. Gold is not pure belief. It is scarce by physics, costly to extract, worn as jewelry, vaulted by central banks, liquid everywhere, recognized by centuries of law and custom. Belief is one layer of the premium, not the whole tower. What survives the correction is narrower and more useful: some real share of gold's value is coordination around a durable symbol, a habit millennia deep — and that history is not decoration on the price. It is the accumulated proof that the agreement survives wars and centuries, which is the one thing a new candidate cannot buy at any price.

Bitcoin proved a complementary set of facts: that scarcity can be enforced by consensus instead of physics, that settlement can be digital and permissionless, that a monetary agreement can bootstrap from nothing if the rules are fixed and anyone can audit them. What bitcoin could not import was history. It spent fifteen years buying some, one survived obituary at a time.

Put side by side, the two look like halves — history without code, code without history — and it is tempting to slide the twelve into the gap. Old names, new rails. Resist that in daylight: nothing about wearing a culture's name transfers the culture's credibility. That transfer is the entire open question of this page, so the comparison below does its bookkeeping honestly and marks the unproven cells unproven.

One newer pressure belongs in the theory, held as a hypothesis and nothing more. Software now makes symbols for free — mascots, brands, whole movements' worth of content by morning. If documented provenance gains salience in that world — *if* — then twenty-four centuries of receipts are worth keeping, and cataloguing them is worth someone's time. That is the whole claim. Demand does not follow from it, and this page does not pretend it does.

The last decade also ran the largest open test of belief-backed value ever attempted: thousands of symbols proposed as coordination points, most now inactive. No failure rate gets quoted here — nobody has defined the cohort. But the wreckage sorts, and it sorts into four piles.

**Someone owns it.** A coin built on a brand, a character, a celebrity borrows a symbol with a legal owner attached, and carries that owner as counterparty forever after. No single party owns the twelve-sign vocabulary. Particular marks, artworks, and implementations can still be protected — the claim covers the underlying names, not everything wearing them.

**Someone worships it.** Sacred vocabulary makes an asset unwelcome to somebody by construction. The sign names are less entangled with any proprietor or organized faith than most symbols strong enough to build on — not universally welcome (some faiths object to astrology itself, and Part VIII takes them seriously), and no global measure of objection is offered here.

**Nobody believes it yet.** The sharpest corner of the memecoin era understood that belief itself is the asset — a community choosing its agreement deliberately is doing openly what every market does tacitly. True, and expensive: acting on it from a standing start means manufacturing a myth and then holding it up forever — content, ritual, recruitment, no finish line. The base rate is brutal. The zodiac's recognition needs none of that; the vocabulary renews on its own calendar, maintained by publishers who have never heard of this page. But the inheritance stops at the vocabulary. Recognition of a mint, trust in its administration, willingness to hold it — all of that is uncovered. The congregation exists. Nothing about it has chosen this ledger.

**There's nothing more to say.** Most token narratives are one premise retold until it stops being funny, and attention for them has to be purchased for as long as they live. Astrology's material renews without being asked — dailies, charts, seasons, a hundred and forty-four pairings — written by independent publishers on their own schedules, none of it needing a token to exist. That breadth belongs to the culture, not to this registry or its operator's platform, and it establishes no demand for anything. It changes one narrow thing: the subject does not run out.

Which brings the sentence that keeps this page honest: **the twelve signs are scarce; tokens named after them are not.** Anyone can deploy a thirteenth Leo this afternoon, and eventually someone will. No code on any chain prevents it, and the openness runs deeper than copycats — the commercial surface around the zodiac (apps, NFTs, wrappers, brands, competing registries) is unlimited. A closed vocabulary is not a closed market. What it provides is twelve familiar names for coordination to land on — cheaper than an open genre where every launch dilutes the last, never automatic, and labels select *names*, not *mints*. The wager of this registry is that convergence happens and lands here. Part VI prices that wager in public instead of leaving it as rhetoric.

(The recurring "thirteenth sign" panic, for what it's worth, keeps resolving back to twelve. Resilience: observed. Mechanism: unproven. Benefit to any token: none shown.)

## Part IV. Names are not addresses.

The hardest question this candidacy faces is not whether the zodiac matters. It is why mattering would pick these mints. A culture cannot endorse a contract. So the mechanisms have to be named, and each one carries its own weakness in plain sight.

**A dated head start.** These records have a continuous public history: origin receipts from 5 July 2024, a registry file, an archive that has not broken. No earlier dated public registry of one-token-per-sign records is known here — produce one, and the claim dies. A priority claim with its own kill switch, not the result of an exhaustive census. Provenance makes a record easy to verify and cite; it compels no one to cite it.

**Cheap to adopt.** The registry file is static and public, the [SDK](https://zodiacs.org/sdk/) open-source, the records machine-readable. A builder who wants sign records can integrate these in an afternoon — and can fork the format and point it at different mints in the same afternoon. The openness cuts both ways, on purpose.

**Twelve obvious labels.** Coordination lands faster on names everyone knows. But the step from name to address is precisely the step the candidacy has to earn, and nothing in the culture takes it automatically.

**And everything that could beat all three.** A better-capitalized issuer. An exchange or wallet shipping its own sign records to a hundred million users. A future registry that out-earns this one on the same criteria — which the criteria would then crown, by design. Or the frontrunner on current evidence: no convergence at all.

What holding a record means today should be said just as plainly. Each sign has a public record with a published address; a Leo can hold *Leo*. The chain verifies the holding, not the Leo — anyone can buy all twelve, and a Leo can hold none. The token is a badge, not a credential: a tradable symbol of affiliation, like a jersey, which never proved fandom either and never needed to. A wallet does not prove a birthday, and this page will not pretend it does. Beyond that, the honest inventory is short. The records are machine-readable. The operator's own consumer surfaces ([Astrofolio](https://astrofolio.xyz/)) gate some features on them — activity the candidacy's rules discount as affiliated, which tells you how strict the rules are, because it is currently most of the activity. Unaffiliated integrations: zero, dated, on the scoreboard. Proposed features are excluded from the claim entirely. Whether holders come to wear the records as public badges is a question the evidence file will answer over time, not something to assert. And some holders are speculating — of course they are; every memetic asset carries that motive. It counts as evidence of nothing, and this page does not appeal to it.

The money around the culture is real, and all of it is service revenue. [Allied Market Research estimated](https://www.alliedmarketresearch.com/press-release/astrology-market.html) the global astrology market at $12.8 billion in 2021 and forecast $22.8 billion by 2031 — an estimate and a forecast, cited as what they are. [Sensor Tower estimated](https://sensortower.com/blog/astrology-apps-2019-revenue-downloads) the ten highest-earning U.S. astrology apps took $39.7 million in consumer spending in 2019, up 64.7 percent on the prior year's top ten. [Co-Star raised a $15 million Series A](https://www.axios.com/2021/04/14/astrology-app-co-star-raises-15-million-funding) for an app that sells horoscopes. Every dollar bought a reading, a subscription, a report: services consumed, nothing held. A paying culture proves the culture pays. About these records, it proves nothing. And [Pew adds](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/05/21/3-in-10-americans-consult-astrology-tarot-cards-or-fortune-tellers/) the right-sized caveat: most people who consult astrology do it mostly for fun, almost none for major decisions. Fun's effect on holding, in either direction: unmeasured.

The twelve have a public history now — short, thin, and real. It reads like this: dated, adverse entries included, receipts for every line in the published evidence register.

**6 July 2024 onward.** Public trading, documented from the day after origin by unaffiliated traders posting rankings and commentary. Low-volume, trader-centric. It establishes that the records were discoverable, and nothing more.

**Mid-July 2024.** Concentration criticism inside the first two weeks — public clustering claims about holder distribution — answered publicly by the affiliated project account. Both sides preserved. Current per-sign concentration is not argued in prose; it sits in the instrument, dated.

**2024–2026, continuously.** Affiliated accounts published throughout. Unaffiliated discussion of the specific addresses stayed sparse and non-recurring across the whole window, and the quiet stretches drew public dismissals — the archive preserves outsiders calling the position a dead bag. Those entries stay. A record that only kept its flattering days would not be a record.

**17 February 2025.** The accident from the top of this page: during the news cycle around an unrelated token also named Libra, a widely followed account bought the registry-listed Libra mint by mistake, called it the wrong token and a meme headed to zero, and later framed the purchase in astrology terms. An accident, described by its maker as one, reported here with its unflattering framing intact — receipts in the evidence register.

## Part VI. The wager, in writing.

This registry claims neither scarcity nor, yet, canonicity. It has no formal authority; any claim to be canonical depends entirely on later independent adoption. What it holds today is dated provenance and a head start — the continuous record since 5 July 2024, machine-readable addresses, an [SDK](https://zodiacs.org/sdk/) any builder can verify against. Until better evidence accumulates, "canonical" names the objective, not the fact.

Five kinds of evidence count, each measured separately, each with a dated pass condition and a dated loss condition, no causal chain among them assumed: independent integrations that read this registry rather than another; third parties citing these addresses unprompted; holder dispersion widening; liquidity that survives quiet months; an archive that stays continuous through adverse ones.

The exclusions do more work than the thresholds, because four of the five gauges can be gamed. Only activity attributable to unaffiliated parties counts toward any pass condition. Integrations, citations, claims, wallets, and liquidity connected to the operator, related parties, paid placement, grants, rebates, coordinated campaigns, or operator-controlled products — the Astrofolio surfaces included — are reported separately and excluded, with the exclusion decision for each observation recorded in the evidence file. Prompted mentions are recorded as prompted. And the fifth gauge, archive continuity, is the weakest by construction — it is under operator control. Losing it would end the candidacy. Keeping it proves diligence, nothing more.

Today the gauges read mostly zeros and baselines, dated, and the loss condition is symmetric: a competing record that out-earns this one on unaffiliated evidence takes the position, and this page will record that too.

One concession bounds the test. The friction explanation cannot absorb disconfirmation forever. So the registry committed to a defined version: a one-tap claim surface for sign-holders, preregistered in the card below — cohort, claimant definition, retention arithmetic, dispersion metric, thresholds, dates, all fixed before the test began, result published either way. A null result kills the specific friction story this page tells; it does not kill every friction story anyone could tell, and this page won't pretend otherwise. A positive result establishes the preregistered outcomes and not one thing more. A thesis that cannot lose is not a thesis.

## Part VIII. The case against.

An argument that hides its weaknesses is an advertisement. Seven objections, each with its evidence and its implication left standing.

**"Belief in the sign is not demand for the record."** Correct, and it is the actual bet. No public measure on this page estimates current demand for the records; the spending in Part IV bought services, not assets; the substitutes are infinitely reproducible. The nearest precedent cuts against: football fan tokens — durable tribal identity, closed sets, official issuance — have shed most of their peak value across the sector's own retrospectives. The structural difference is narrow. Fan tokens were centrally issued against promised utility that mostly never arrived; the twelve are permissionless records that promise nothing — no votes, no perks, no yield. Less to fail to deliver, and less to point to. The implication, unhedged: this page establishes a falsifiable candidacy. It does not estimate a probability of success, and it does not claim the probability is high.

**"If the audience wanted this, it would already exist."** The crypto-astrology overlap is a partial test with a negative result, and it counts against the thesis. What remains is narrower: the preregistered test puts a defined mainstream cohort in front of a specified one-tap flow — something the record shows that cohort has not had — and if nothing moves, the friction explanation is spent and this page says so. Implication: the thesis currently rests on an untested mechanism, and the test date is published.

**"The registry appointed itself."** It did. Some registries begin without formal authority and become authoritative through use; most never do. Legitimacy here can only be earned slowly — publish verifiable facts, keep the archive continuous, never sell the position, be right for years — and the criteria are symmetric: the evidence that would crown this record would crown a competitor that out-earned it. Implication: self-appointment is not disqualifying, and it is not evidence either.

**"This is a memecoin with a literature review."** The token is memetic — priced by attention and belief — and the literature review does not change that. The open question is whether a fixed vocabulary changes address-level coordination. Implication: the insult and the thesis describe the same object; the difference is the level of care, and care is what this page has to offer.

**"You are describing a security and insisting you are not."** The registry's answer is behavioral and auditable: it issues nothing, sells nothing, custodies nothing, promises nothing — no yield, no buyback, no roadmap of managerial effort for a holder to rely on. The catalogue analogy has a stated limit: this operator holds the catalogued assets and builds adjacent products, which is exactly why the [disclosures](https://zodiacs.org/disclosure/) exist and why Part VI discounts operator-adjacent activity. Implication: the page publishes facts and labels arguments, and asks to be judged on whether those two registers ever blur.

**"One operator runs the registry, the companion app, and holds the assets it catalogues."** True, attested, published, dated, in the operator's own words. Part V adds the uncomfortable detail: the earliest affiliated language was investment sloganeering, and the registry voice came second. The structural mitigation is replaceability — static file, forkable SDK, mirrored archive, symmetric criteria. Public files lower the technical cost of routing around an operator; they do not guarantee anyone would bother. Implication: operator concentration is a live risk to price, not a solved one.

**"The essay is the mythology; the instrument is the asset."** The sharpest of the seven, and the only one prose cannot answer. Most readers cannot reconstruct control rights from a block explorer, and should not have to. The answer is Part VII, and the implication is an instruction: weigh the table more than the prose. Where they ever disagree, the table wins.

## Part IX. The size of the claim.

The title of this page is an ambition, the way "canonical" is. Here is the claim at its actual size.

Inherited recognition may spare one coordination cost, for one kind of asset. It selects no addresses and creates no demand. Whether unaffiliated parties converge on these records is not a matter of argument anymore — it is a matter of record, kept in public: criteria published, exclusions defined, test preregistered, result binding either way. Two years of unbroken documentation is the head start. The scoreboard is mostly zeros. Both stay on this page on purpose.

The registry's product is not conviction. It is documentation — kept where anyone can check it, against criteria published before the answer was known. The culture never needed these records, and never will. Whether these records come to matter is what the test is for. The test can say no.

# Appendix — The technical record

*Kept apart so the essay reads and the record verifies. Everything below is maintained with the same care as the argument above.*

## A. Reading the instrument.

The column legend for the instrument table in Part VII:

- **Supply** — total and circulating, per sign
- **Mint authority** — status per sign (retained / renounced), address if retained
- **Freeze authority** — status per sign
- **Holder concentration** — top-10 wallet share per sign, dated
- **Liquidity** — venue, pool addresses, LP ownership and any locks
- **Bridge** — mechanism for Base representations, custody model, what fails if the bridge fails
- **Treasury and insider holdings** — project-controlled wallets, amounts, any lockups or vesting, disposal policy
- **Registry continuity** — governance of the record itself; what survives if the operating entity disappears (static registry file, forkable SDK, mirrored archive)

Statuses are three-valued and mean what they say: **verified** (a stranger can check the receipt), **operator-attested** (the operator states it, dated, not independently verified), **pending** (neither, and labeled so). Pending is never dressed as verified.

## B. Why Solana. Why Base.

The native records were deployed on Solana. The relevant selection factors are technical and practical: transaction cost, confirmation behavior, consumer wallet availability, existing liquidity venues, and the operator's existing infrastructure at deployment time. Official bridged representations extend each sign to Base through the Wormhole lock-and-mint contracts listed per sign in the instrument. The Base representations trace to the specified Solana mints through those bridge contracts, and their validity depends on the bridge's stated contracts and trust assumptions. If the bridge fails, the native Solana records stand unaffected; the bridged representations are conveniences, not the record.

## C. Methodology and evidence.

The Pulse reads open sources on a published schedule: encyclopedia pageviews from the Wikimedia API (live, with a weekly committed snapshot as fallback) and search interest from public Trends data, with the comparison set and its limits stated inline. Pageviews measure curiosity, not bids; the essay never uses them as demand.

The candidacy scoreboard and the one-tap test read their committed JSON registers, which carry measurement definitions, capture times, exclusions, and raw captures in the published [evidence file](https://zodiacs.org/thesis/thesis-candidacy-evidence.json). Every observation carries an independence classification (unsolicited-unaffiliated, unsolicited-unknown, prompted, campaign, affiliated) and an economic-interest classification; only unsolicited-unaffiliated observations count toward pass conditions, and each exclusion decision is recorded. The public-history events in Part V carry the same schema — post identifiers, capture times, artifact grades A–E, live/deleted status — in the same register family. The test is preregistered: criteria, cohort, thresholds, and dates were fixed and published before the test began, and the result publishes either way.

## D. Verify it yourself.

Each instrument row links to an independently checkable receipt, or is labeled operator-attested or pending. Mint addresses and origin transactions are on a public explorer; pool and holder data are on chain; the registry file is at [/registry/zodiacs.registry.json](https://zodiacs.org/registry/zodiacs.registry.json); the [SDK](https://zodiacs.org/sdk/) reads it all programmatically. The [disclosure register](https://zodiacs.org/disclosure/) carries the operator statements. If a claim on this page and a receipt ever disagree, the receipt wins, and the page is wrong until corrected.
