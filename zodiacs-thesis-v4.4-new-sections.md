## Part IV. Who this is for.

Anyone with a birthday has a sign. The people who use theirs are a narrower
group, and it is worth being precise about who they are. In a [Pew Research
Center survey fielded in October 2024](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/05/21/3-in-10-americans-consult-astrology-tarot-cards-or-fortune-tellers/),
30% of U.S. adults said they consult astrology, tarot cards, or a fortune
teller at least once a year. Among women ages 18 to 49, 46% consult astrology
or a horoscope that often. The habit concentrates in adults under 50, and in
women most of all.

Pew also measured how seriously people take it. 20% of adults engage with at
least one of these practices mostly for fun, 10% mostly because they find the
insights helpful, and just 1% rely a lot on what they learn when making major
decisions. For most of this audience the sign is a ritual and a shared
reference, not an oracle. Nothing on this page requires it to be more. A
birthday post works the same whether or not anyone believes Mercury does
anything.

For adults under 50, the sign already lives in digital places. It sits in
dating profiles and app bios, gets compared in group chats, and arrives each
morning through apps; [Co-Star alone reports 30 million registered
accounts](https://www.statista.com/statistics/1451873/costar-registered-users/),
a company-reported figure. These are people who keep money, tickets, and
photographs on a phone. A sign held in a wallet asks for a gesture they already
make every day.

Capital is moving toward the same cohorts. [Cerulli Associates estimates nearly
$124 trillion](https://www.ml.com/articles/great-wealth-transfer-impact.html)
in U.S. assets will change hands through 2048, about $106 trillion of it going
to heirs, primarily Gen X, millennials, and Gen Z. In [Bank of America's 2024
Study of Wealthy Americans](https://www.ml.com/articles/great-wealth-transfer-impact.html),
72% of surveyed investors ages 21 to 43 said above-average returns are no
longer possible from traditional stocks and bonds alone; 28% of older investors
agreed, and the younger group reported a stronger preference for crypto,
digital assets, and private equity. That study samples wealthy investors, not a
generation, and inherited wealth stays concentrated in few families.

None of this is evidence that astrology readers will buy digital assets, and
this page does not claim it. A horoscope habit is not a portfolio decision, a
survey answer is not a purchase, and a wealth-transfer projection promises
nothing to any particular asset. The numbers establish something narrower. The
twelve names do their daily work inside the cohorts most at home holding
digital property, which makes those cohorts the natural test population. It
does not make them buyers.

Whether the overlap converts is a measured question. The [candidacy
scoreboard](#the-candidacy) counts independent adoption, and the [preregistered
one-tap test](#the-test) puts claim, keep, and spread on a deadline. If those
cohorts never show up in the numbers, this section described an audience and
nothing more.

## Part VIII. Against manufactured belief.

Most new tokens die young. [CoinGecko counts a token as
failed](https://www.coingecko.com/research/publications/how-many-cryptocurrencies-failed)
once it was listed, traded at least once, and no longer trades. By that
definition, 53.2% of the nearly 20.2 million tokens listed on GeckoTerminal
since 2021 have failed, and 11.6 million of those failures came in 2025 alone,
the year launchpads reduced creating a token to filling in a form.

[Chainalysis read the 2024
launches](https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/crypto-market-manipulation-wash-trading-pump-and-dump-2025/)
and found the same shape. More than 2 million tokens launched that year, and at
the time of its January 2025 report only 1.7% were still actively traded in the
previous 30 days. Tokens showing pump-and-dump patterns, 3.59% of launches,
were abandoned after six to seven days on average.

That mortality follows from the starting position. A new memecoin begins with
nothing but a ticker. No one recognizes its name, no calendar brings it back,
and no ritual repeats it. Its creators have to invent a character, rent
attention for it, and hope belief takes hold before the audience drifts. The
base rates above say how often that works.

The twelve assets in this registry started from the opposite position. The
names they carry have been in circulation for roughly 2,400 years; nobody has
to be taught them, and no marketing budget keeps them alive. The horoscopes,
birthday posts, and sign seasons measured earlier on this page arrive on
schedule whether or not any asset exists. The assets inherit that recognition
and nothing else. Recognition supplies an audience. Demand still has to be
earned.

Set the record against the base rate, carefully. The twelve mints date from 5
July 2024, with trading pools created the same day, per Dexscreener. At the
most recent capture, 23 July 2026, all twelve tracked pools were live and the
project archive remained continuous. The same capture shows the size: $11.9k to
$17.8k of pool depth per sign, and $10 to $158 of daily volume. By the
dead-coin definitions above, the twelve count as alive after two years. By
size, they are small markets. Both readings are true, and the scoreboard below
carries both.

Two years against a base rate measured in days looks favorable, and this page
declines to grade it. No matched cohort of July 2024 launches is maintained
here, so no survival percentile is claimed. Persistence shows the record was
kept: pools live, archive unbroken, disclosures dated. It does not show new
people arrived; the scoreboard below still records no independent integrations
and no unprompted citations. The dated history above keeps the dismissals too:
"dead bag" commentary sits on the same timeline as the receipts.

A memecoin opens a market first, then searches for a product people might want
inside it: a character, a joke, a community that stays. The zodiac ran in the
other order. People used the product daily long before 5 July 2024, and the
market for holding a piece of it is the new part.
[Astrofolio](https://astrofolio.xyz/), a related consumer project under
separate control, is one live attempt at that conversion, a place where the
product is meant to become the market. Under this page's rules its activity
counts for nothing: related projects are excluded from the adoption evidence,
and only strangers count. Whether strangers choose this record is what the
[preregistered test below](#the-test) measures, and the result publishes either
way.

## 6. Slop self-check

Patterns caught and removed while drafting:

1. Binary-contrast snap — an early close read "The audience is real. The
   demand is not." Folded into declaratives: "The numbers establish something
   narrower…" and "Recognition supplies an audience. Demand still has to be
   earned." (calibration in E1's own idiom, not a reveal).
2. Fake precision — nearly generalized Chainalysis's 6–7-day abandonment to
   all launches; it applies only to the suspected pump-and-dump cohort, and is
   now scoped to it.
3. Mic-drop fragment — "Usually the audience moves on." replaced with "The
   base rates above say how often that works." (routes to receipts).
4. Dramatic colon reveal — "The direction is still clear: …" deleted; the
   concession paragraph states the overlap plainly. Remaining colons introduce
   lists or definitions only.
5. Superlative without a receipt — "the highest rate of any group Pew
   reports" (not verbatim in the report) softened to "in women most of all."
6. Overclaim the prior review already killed — "two years of continuous
   pools" would contradict E1's "continuous historical depth was not
   archived"; the draft claims creation date plus live-at-capture instead, and
   declines a survival percentile (no matched cohort).
7. Em dashes — none in either draft; periods, commas, parentheses. Banned-word
   sweep (delve, robust, leverage, etc.): zero hits.
8. Guru closer — the memecoin section ends on the test's mechanics ("the
   result publishes either way"), with the "product becomes the market" line
   carried by the labeled Astrofolio sentence two lines earlier, exclusion
   attached.
